Serve: which side?

A first serve takes under half a second to land. The returner can't see it in time — they have to guess. So a server's worst fear is being read: serve one side every time and the returner leans there.

The answer comes from game theory. In 1928 von Neumann proved that in a two-player, win-or-lose contest, once you're allowed to mix, there is a balance point neither side can exploit — and his example was rock-paper-scissors. In 1950 Nash generalized it: the Nash equilibrium, later honoured with a Nobel Prize.

For serving, the best plan is to mix until wide and T give the same chance to win the point. Mixing isn't 50–50: on the ad side Sinner serves wide 58.7% of the time and down the T 35.4%, yet wins 69.7% vs 71.1% — no statistical difference.

We redid the papers' test on 2,151 men's matches since 2023 (Match Charting Project, volunteer-charted, not official): wide 65.0%, T 64.7%; of 1,056 groups (same server, same returner, same court side) only 5.3% deviate — about what chance alone gives (5%). On game points, break points and match points, still no difference.

But a 2025 study in the Journal of Political Economy that adds the score finds most top players don't fully get there.

von Neumann put it this way: at one-half each, no one can predict which side he'll choose — not even himself. The best serve is one even you don't know is coming.

Do you keep throwing the same thing in rock-paper-scissors?
