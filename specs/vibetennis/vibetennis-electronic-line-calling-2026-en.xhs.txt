Electronic line calling: how accurate is it?

Take Hawk-Eye, the most widely used system. The oval you see touching the line in a replay isn't filmed — it's calculated. In every frame, each camera finds the centre of the ball; where their lines of sight meet is the ball's position in 3D. One point per frame, joined into a trajectory, from which the system models the ball's "footprint" on the court.

Why model it? The cameras run at up to 340 frames per second, about 3 ms apart — a 200 km/h ball travels about 16 cm between frames — while the ball touches the ground for only about 5 ms. The bounce itself gets one or two frames at most. Dropped straight down, the contact patch is up to about 4 cm; landing fast at an angle it squashes and slides, and in experiments the footprint stretched to about 10 cm. That footprint is never measured directly. Even the mark on the court can't be trusted: on clay it can be larger than the real contact, on hard courts smaller. Roland-Garros still checks the mark.

And the human eye? A study of 1,473 challenges found line judges' positional uncertainty is around 2 cm, about 3 cm near the baseline; 39% of challenged calls were wrong. The ITF's top certification requires test balls more than 5 mm from the line to be called 100% correctly.

In or out is decided by the system and can't go to video review; review covers only things like double bounces, touches and net calls (US Open since 2023, Wimbledon since 2026). Foot faults are still judged by a person watching the feed.

It isn't infallible: within 5 mm is permitted error; a surface just 1 mm uneven can shift a fast serve's landing by 3–4 mm; and at Wimbledon 2025 the system was switched off for a game by mistake.
