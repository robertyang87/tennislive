Why is clay slow and grass fast?

Hit the same ball at the same speed onto clay, grass and a hard court, and three different balls come off the ground. That is why the four Grand Slams play like four different games — Nadal won Roland-Garros 14 times on clay.

The secret is in the few milliseconds of contact: the bottom of the ball slides forward first, friction drags on it, and soon the court "grips" it. That decides how much horizontal speed the ball keeps; the other half is bounce — how hard the court pushes the ball back up.

At low angles clay's friction can reach 0.9: the fine grit is like ball bearings under a player's shoes but like sandpaper on the ball's felt, and the ball digs a small dent and kicks up higher. Wimbledon grass is only 0.5–0.6, so the ball skids on, low and fast.

Hard courts are not all the same. They are a few layers of acrylic coating, and pace depends on the sand in the top coat: the US Open's supplier says big, angular sand grabs the felt and slows the ball; finer, rounder sand makes it quicker. The Australian Open uses another maker's coating and can change its roughness each year. In 2021 Djokovic called it the fastest court in his 15 years there; organisers said pre-event testing was about the same as the year before.

From ATP match stats since 1991 we computed a serve index for each Slam (above 1 means more aces than the players' usual level predicts). Last decade: Roland-Garros 0.75, US Open 1.02, Wimbledon 1.09, Australian Open 1.12. By friction Wimbledon grass is fastest, but by aces the Australian Open gives the most — and its index rose from 1.02 to 1.19 in 2021.

People say Wimbledon slowed after switching grass in 2001. Wimbledon says the move to 100% ryegrass was for durability and did not change perceived pace; our data shows no slowdown either. The odd year out is Roland-Garros 2020: moved to autumn, cold, with a new ball — serve index 0.60, the lowest of the decade.

The court is a player in the match too.
