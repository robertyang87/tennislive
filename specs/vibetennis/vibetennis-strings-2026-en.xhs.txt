Did the strings change tennis?

1874: sheep gut. Then nylon. 1997: Kuerten wins Roland-Garros on polyester; by 2010 every man at Wimbledon used it.

Strings can change only three things: power, spin and tension.

Power — restitution: the ratio of the ball's speed off the strings to its speed in. Higher means a faster ball. But in the lab every string returns about 95% of the energy, and on court 180 N vs 280 N strings changed groundstroke speed by only about 3%. Strings did not make the ball faster.

Spin — Coulomb friction: brushed at an angle, the ball slides first and friction drags it into spin. Peer-reviewed tests found every string grips enough that the ball is rolling before it leaves the strings, so more friction adds no spin. What adds spin is the main strings sliding and snapping back — the 1977 "spaghetti" racket did that for about 2x the spin and was banned in 1978. Polyester? A retailer's test says ~20% more spin than nylon; peer-reviewed tests find no material effect.

Tension — stress relaxation: tension falls about 30% right after stringing, fastest for polyester. Looser strings hold the ball longer and launch it higher — long. So pros take a freshly strung racket at every ball change.

Playing style: from Match Charting Project men's data (volunteer-charted, aces excluded), serve-and-volley fell from about 40% of points in the 1990s to under 10% after 2005. Heavier topspin makes returns dip at the volleyer's feet and passing shots faster; in 2010–18 the same servers won 66.8% of serve-and-volley points vs 72.2% staying back (Sackmann). Analysts point to polyester topspin — inferred from timing; rackets and balls changed too, and no study has isolated the strings.
