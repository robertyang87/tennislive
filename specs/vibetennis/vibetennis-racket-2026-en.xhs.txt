Did the racket change tennis?

For nearly a century, wooden rackets had a strung area of about 70 square inches. 1963: René Lacoste's steel-racket patent, licensed to Wilson as the T2000. 1976: Howard Head's oversize patent. Around 1980: carbon fibre takes over.

Head's problem was specific: on old rackets the "no-jar" point sat near the throat, while most players hit the middle of the face. He didn't move the point — he stretched the face toward the handle until it was inside.

The "sweet spot" is really three points: the fastest-rebound point, the vibration node, and the no-jar centre of percussion. The best feel is usually the node.

A wider head twists less on an off-centre hit. In experiments the ball barely changes direction but comes off clearly slower; in a real swing it also flies higher and longer — so miss-hits tend to sail long.

Stiffer frames mostly help off-centre hits and serves. Lighter rackets swing faster, but only a little: 10% less swing weight, about 3% more swing speed.

An ITF researcher wrote that modern rackets moved the game from "technique" to "power and spin". The ITF's own explanation: a bigger head gives better control of off-centre hits; a stiffer frame absorbs less energy, so the ball comes off faster. But as with strings, balls changed in the same years, and no study has isolated the racket's effect.
